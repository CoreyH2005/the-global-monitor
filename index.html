Trump, America, and Tariffs

Between January and April 2025, President Donald Trump introduced a series of tariffs on imports from multiple countries during the early months of his second presidential administration. Public discourse surrounding these tariff policies has largely been shaped by political ideology — supporters have defended them under the "America First" agenda, while critics have characterised them as economically damaging and harmful to international trade relations. This piece sets that framing aside to look at what the tariffs have actually done: to trade, to prices, and to the deficit they were built to fix.

A tool with a long history. Tariffs — taxes imposed by governments on imported goods — have played a role in American economic policy for over a century, historically used to protect domestic industries and generate government revenue. Major tariff increases have occurred before, including the Payne–Aldrich Tariff Act, the Fordney–McCumber Tariff Act, and the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act. The Trump administration's key justification this time is the United States' persistent trade deficit: the U.S. currently runs one exceeding $900 billion, among the largest in the world, and the tariffs are intended to narrow that gap by making foreign goods more expensive relative to domestic production.

What actually happened to rates and revenue. Since the beginning of Trump's second term, the average tariff rate has increased significantly, rising from approximately 2.4 percent to 16.8 percent by the end of 2025. Tariff revenue rose with it: the United States collected approximately $287 billion in tariff revenue during 2025, a 192 percent increase compared to 2024.

"Despite this rise in government revenue, economists widely argue that tariffs impose costs on domestic consumers and businesses."

When companies are required to pay tariffs on imported goods or raw materials, those additional costs are often passed on to consumers through higher prices. Data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics indicates that prices have increased for several categories of consumer goods, including food products, coffee, and household furnishings. The industries most heavily impacted include automobiles, auto parts, and steel manufacturing.

Who actually pays — and the legal cloud over it. Tariffs also carry indirect effects on the labour market. Higher import costs raise production expenses for businesses reliant on global supply chains, and recent labour market indicators suggest a weakening job market, with the ratio of job openings to unemployed workers falling below 0.9 — its lowest level since the pandemic-era disruption of early 2021. Legal challenges have complicated implementation further: the Supreme Court has ruled that a significant share of these tariffs — those imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — were enacted without proper legal authority, opening the door to refund claims and leaving a meaningful share of 2025's tariff revenue in genuine legal limbo.

Trump's tariff policies represent a significant shift in U.S. trade policy, emphasising protectionism and domestic economic priorities. They have increased government revenue and were framed around narrowing the trade deficit, but they have also contributed to rising consumer prices, uncertainty for businesses, and now a live legal challenge to their own foundation. The long-term picture depends on how trading partners respond, how the courts settle the refund question, and whether future administrations continue pursuing similar protectionist policy.
